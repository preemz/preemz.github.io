Importing thousands of candidates without breaking a single record

Primawan Satrio · August 2026 · ~9 min read
Update, November 2026: This post originally skipped the commit step, so it is covered below. Importing now requires a lawful-basis attestation that is stored with the import, the admin picks an internal owner or leaves people in the follow-up queue, and uploads are capped at 20 MB. The importer reads 24 fields rather than the five in the alias table, including marital status, last university, GPA and salary details added in October 2026. I also corrected what the post said about who can use the importer, the size of the parser, and how the duplicate lookups run.

When we moved our recruiting operation onto our own platform, the hardest part was not the job board or the application flow. It was the database: years of candidate records living in a Lark base, with column names in a mix of English and Bahasa Indonesia, phone numbers formatted six different ways, and the same people entered more than once by different recruiters. A naive import would have created phantom duplicates that our team would chase for months.

This post covers the CSV importer we built to solve that: a forgiving parser, smart column mapping, and a dry-run preview that shows exactly what will happen to every row before anything is written. The feature now lives at /admin/import, and our internal staff (admins and the internal team) use it whenever a client or partner sends over a spreadsheet of candidates. Freelance recruiters cannot open it.

Parsing: assume the file is worse than it is

Spreadsheet exports are hostile. Real files we received contained a UTF-8 byte-order mark, CRLF line endings from Windows Excel, quoted fields with embedded commas and newlines, doubled quotes inside quoted fields, and trailing blank lines. Rather than pull in a dependency on a Workers-sized budget, we wrote a ~40-line RFC 4180 parser that handles all of it:

  • Byte-order mark stripping so the first header is not silently corrupted into ï»¿email
  • Proper quote state machine with escaped-quote handling ("" inside a quoted field)
  • Blank-row filtering and whitespace trimming on every cell
Design rule: every quirk in this parser exists because a real export contained it. When your parser meets a new quirk in production, fix the parser, not the file.

Column mapping: meet the spreadsheet where it is

The next problem is that exports never use your field names. Our Lark base called the email column everything from Email Address to e-mail to surel (Indonesian for email). Instead of forcing a rigid template, the importer guesses the mapping using an alias table, with Indonesian aliases as first-class citizens. The fields grew over time: marital status, last university, GPA and salary details (currency, period, gross or net) were added in October 2026.

The table shows five of the 24 importable fields. Header matching is done on a normalized form: lowercased, everything but letters and digits stripped, so "E-Mail Address" matches "emailaddress". When no header matches exactly, hints longer than three characters are also tried as substrings.

The guesser has two rules that matter. First, it never maps the same column twice: once a header is claimed by a field, it is removed from the pool, so two similar columns cannot both feed one field. Second, every guess is shown to the admin on a review screen where any choice can be overridden before committing. The machine proposes; a human disposes.

The dry-run: no surprises allowed

The part I am most insistent on is that no candidate record is created until the admin has seen, row by row, what will happen. The upload lands on a review page showing every row classified into one of five actions:

  • create: valid, unknown email, will be imported
  • invalid: missing or malformed email, or missing name; excluded with the reason shown
  • duplicateInFile: same email appeared on an earlier line of this same file
  • alreadyRegistered: this person already has an account on the platform
  • ownedByRecruiter: the person sits in a recruiter's talent pool with an active ownership window; the preview names the recruiter and the expiry date

Email is the identity key for all of it. Everything deduplicates on the normalized (lowercased) email address, because in our business the email is the one field that reliably identifies a person across systems, while names collide and phone numbers change format.

The commit step

Pressing Import is a separate step with its own rules, not a click-through:

  • Lawful-basis attestation. The admin must tick a box confirming a lawful basis for the records, that the people were told their data is held for recruitment, and that the file is a migration of an existing database rather than newly sourced data. The import stores the timestamp (attestedAt) and the exact wording shown (attestationText).
  • An owner, or the follow-up queue. Whoever runs the import picks an internal team member as owner for the new records, or leaves them unassigned, in which case they land in the follow-up queue. Ownership is never assigned automatically.
  • Rows are inserted one at a time, only the create rows, after the preview is recomputed with the submitted mapping. Imported people get a random password nobody knows, so they take over their account through the normal registration flow.
  • Uploads are capped at 20 MB, and a file with no data rows is refused before anything is stored.

Performance: two passes, and what they do not fix

The deduplication check needs the database: a lookup for registered users and another for recruiter ownership, per row. On Cloudflare Workers a request has a limited budget of subrequests, so the preview separates the file-only work from the database work in two passes:

  1. Synchronous first pass over the file itself: validate emails, require names, detect in-file duplicates with a set. No database involved, and ordering stays strict so "same email appears earlier in this file" is always accurate.
  2. Concurrent second pass over the surviving rows: each row fires its own lookup against registered users and recruiter ownerships, all started at once with Promise.all. That is concurrent, not batched. It finishes sooner, but it is still about two queries per row (a third when the person is in a pool), so the subrequest cost grows with the file.

Every import is also recorded with its filename, headers, mapping, and who uploaded it, so a bad import can be audited after the fact rather than wondered about.

I have not measured where the subrequest limit bites for this importer, and the commit step also inserts rows one at a time. For a very large file the workaround is to split it; the fix I would make is to replace the per-row lookups with a few bulk queries.

What this taught us

  • Dedupe on identity, not on rows. The unit of truth is the person (by email), not the spreadsheet line. Getting this wrong is how companies end up calling the same candidate twice from two "different" records.
  • Show the future before you create it. A dry-run preview that classifies every row converts an import from an act of faith into a reviewable decision. The admin who sees "In a recruiter's talent pool until 30/11/2026" trusts the system; the one who sees a spinner does not.
  • Bilingual data needs bilingual tooling. Half our header aliases are Indonesian because our data is Indonesian. Tooling that only understands English column names would have made every import a manual mapping exercise.
  • A ~40-line parser beats a dependency when you know exactly what you must survive. RFC 4180 is small, and owning the code means the next weird export is a one-line fix away.

The importer looks mundane next to LLM pipelines and scoring engines, but it is the feature that made the rest trustworthy: the match engine can only be as good as the candidate data underneath it, and this is how that data got in cleanly.
